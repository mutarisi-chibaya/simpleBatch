Calibration curve

Getting started

  1. Set up the mixture, heater and cooling water on the Lab tab. The defaults follow the lab sheet, so you can leave them as they are for a first run.
  2. Press Start and watch the apparatus, live readings and graphs. Press Finish instantly to skip to the end.
  3. Read your fractions in the table under the graphs, then open Analysis for the refractometer calibration, the Rayleigh equation and the equilibrium diagrams.
  4. Learn why with the Lessons tab. Each lesson changes one thing and explains the result.

How the Rayleigh integral is evaluated: y* comes from the equilibrium model at the lab pressure, and ∫ dx/(y* − x) is integrated from xW to xF by Simpson's rule with 200 intervals. The same integral can be done graphically, as the area under a plot of 1/(y* − x) against x, as shown on the Analysis tab.

About the model

Thermodynamics. The Antoine equation gives pure-component vapour pressures (mmHg, °C). Van Laar activity coefficients give the non-ideality. The constants were fitted to the literature azeotropes at 101.325 kPa (IPA–water x = 0.681 at 80.37 °C, giving A₁₂ = 2.1366 and A₂₁ = 1.1862; ethanol–water x = 0.894 at 78.15 °C, giving A₁₂ = 1.8766 and A₂₁ = 0.9402). Bubble points come from solving Σ γᵢxᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ = P. The fitted ethanol model matches measured equilibrium data closely, for example y = 0.614 at x = 0.40.


Still. The pot is a single well-mixed liquid. During heat-up, dT/dt = (Q − UA(T − 20))/Cp. Once boiling, the net heat sets the boil-up rate, V = Qnet/ΔHvap. The vapour leaving is y = x + E(y* − x), where E is the still efficiency. A high boil-up rate also entrains liquid droplets of pot composition, and the entrained fraction grows with the square of the vapour rate. That is why the procedure turns the heater down. A condenser with low or no cooling water lets part of the vapour escape.


Bumping. Without boiling chips the liquid superheats by about 3 °C before it boils violently. After that it bumps at random, splashing 0.5–2 mL of pot liquid over into the fraction being collected.


Refractometer. Refractive index against mass fraction at 20 °C is interpolated from approximate handbook values. Readings get Gaussian noise at the selected precision. Ethanol–water has a refractive index maximum near 80 wt%, so one reading can match two compositions. The page flags this and resolves it with the still-head temperature, picking the root whose dew point (for fractions) or bubble point (for pot samples) matches.


Volumes. Liquid volumes come from real mixture densities at 20 °C, so mixing alcohol and water contracts. Mixing 125 mL of IPA with 125 mL of water gives about 240 mL. Feed and fraction strengths are quoted as %v/v on the prepared basis (pure volumes before mixing), the same way calibration standards are made.


Condenser holdup. The condenser and adapter hold about 1.2 mL of liquid. The first drop arrives only after the walls are wet, and that liquid never reaches a fraction, so the mole balance closes slightly below 100%.


Sample temperature. Refractive index falls with temperature (about −0.0004 per °C for the alcohols, −0.0001 for water). The standards are read at 20 °C, so reading warm fractions makes them look weaker than they are.


Flask size. The mantle power, heat-up mass and heat-loss area scale with the flask. A flask more than about 70% full foams and sprays liquid into the condenser.


Switching effects off. Every effect above, plus imperfect equilibrium, spray carry-over and heat loss, has its own switch under Real-world effects. With all of them off, the vapour is exactly in equilibrium, volumes add ideally, nothing holds up in the condenser and the refractometer is perfect. That is the textbook still, and it is the case the Rayleigh equation describes.


Calibration. Measured compositions are read off the calibration curve by linear interpolation between neighbouring standards by default, the same as reading a calibration table. A fitted 4th-order polynomial is available for comparison on the Refractometer tab.


Still simplified. The pot is perfectly mixed, the refractive index and density tables are approximate handbook values, and heat capacities and heats of vaporisation do not change with temperature. Use the simulation to understand trends and plan runs. Report your real lab data as your results.
